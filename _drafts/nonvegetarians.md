To those who still eat meat.

This short essay is a plea for you to give up eating meat. I know that this is a big thing to ask you to do; it entails a radical change to the way you have lived and connect with other people. However, I think that this is the only moral way to live life if we are to be entirely honest with ourselves even at the cost that it may incur. This essay is divided into two parts. First, I want to outline what my methodology for moral introspection is. A great many non-vegetarians seem to adopt an anti-realist stance towards moral facts; that there are no moral facts that have the capacity to constrain their actions. The upshot is that their eating meat is just one of the many actions permitted by the world; everything is permitted so to speak. This is how they comfort themselves when it comes to eating meat. What I want to undermine here is not the anti-realist position; that is a different can of worms. Instead, I want to undermine the comfort with which non-vegetarians pronounce that they are fine with them eating meat. The thought is that once the non-vegetarian is forced to confront with the consequences of their eating meat, it would cause them to hesitate from so eagerly endorsing that eating meat is fine. So, even if anti-realism is true, the internal discomfort should give the non-vegetarian a reason to want to find a cure to the ailment. Note that I am not defending any substantial thesis about ethics or metaethics here; I am just probing around the non-vegetarian's conscience to see if the extent they are fine with the discomfort that comes with eating meat. If you find eating meat discomforting, then you should want to not live with that discomfort. Second, I want to argue that if you find the prospect of you eating meat discomforting, the thing that you should do is to adopt vegetarianism. That is, the quickest and easiest cure to this ailment is simply to adopt vegetarianism. If this succeeds, I will have accomplished my goal of convincing you to give up meat. 

# Moral Introspection
To make the case most pointed, I grant the non-vegetarian the most prima facie ethical way to procure and eat meat with our current best technologies. That is, suppose the meat comes from livestock that is raised in the most ideal environment one could think of and killed under anesthesia with no ostensible signs of pain. If I could show that the non-vegetarian should even this most prima facie ethical case discomforting, then I will have achieved my goal. Now, because of how alienated we are from the actual production of meat, even in this ideal case, it is hard to see why it should make the non-vegetarian uncomfortable. Hence, I will assume, to my favor, that the non-vegetarian is to raise and kill the animal themselves. If the prospect of doing it yourself is too discomforting, then it should not matter who does the raising and the killing; if the animal ends up on your plate, you should find it discomforting.
